History of the Hobby

From Advertising Cards
to a Global Hobby

Long before collectors chased rookie cards, autographs, serial numbers, parallels and one-of-ones, trading cards began with a much simpler purpose: advertising.

Over more than 150 years, these small pieces of cardboard have evolved from promotional giveaways tucked inside product packages into historical artifacts, pop-culture icons, investment assets and the foundation of a worldwide collecting community.

Their history is filled with innovation, competition, accidental rarities—and more than a few stories that seem almost impossible today.

Before the Trading Card

The ancestors of today’s trading cards can be traced back hundreds of years.

European merchants were using printed trade cards by at least the late 1600s and early 1700s. They served somewhat like a combination of a modern business card, advertisement and map. Because street numbering was inconsistent—and literacy was far from universal—merchants often incorporated recognizable shop signs and illustrations so customers could find their businesses.

Trade cards eventually reached colonial America and became increasingly common during the 1800s. Advances in color lithography transformed them from simple advertisements into attractive miniature works of art that people actually wanted to save.

By the mid-to-late 19th century, Americans were collecting colorful advertising cards featuring animals, flowers, celebrities, humorous scenes and other subjects and placing them into scrapbooks. (Library of Congress)

Without realizing it, those early collectors were establishing a behavior that remains central to the hobby today:

Find it. Keep it. Trade it. Complete the set.

Baseball Enters the Picture

Photography created another predecessor of the modern trading card: cartes de visite and cabinet cards.

These mounted photographs often depicted individuals, celebrities, teams and athletes. The National Baseball Hall of Fame notes that the first commercially produced baseball-related cards used for advertising appeared during the late 1860s. (Baseball Hall of Fame)

Among the most famous early examples are cards associated with Peck & Snyder, a sporting-goods company that used photographs of baseball teams to promote its business around the era of baseball’s first openly professional team, the Cincinnati Red Stockings.

Baseball had discovered something that would define trading cards for generations: fans didn’t simply want to watch their heroes.

They wanted to own a piece of them.

The Tobacco Card Revolution

The real explosion came during the 1880s.

Tobacco companies began placing collectible cards into cigarette products. Baseball players appeared alongside boxers, actors, actresses, cyclists, circus performers, flags, animals and countless other subjects.

The cards were marketing tools designed to build brand loyalty, but they also sometimes served a practical purpose by helping stiffen soft cigarette packages. Interestingly, the Library of Congress notes that advertising—not package reinforcement—was the primary reason for the cards, since some cigarette packaging soon became rigid enough that stiffeners were unnecessary. (The Metropolitan Museum of Art)

Companies such as Allen & Ginter, Goodwin & Company and W.S. Kimball produced beautifully illustrated sets that collectors still pursue more than a century later.

Allen & Ginter’s late-1880s cards covered subjects far beyond baseball. One 1889 set, for example, featured 50 different styles of decorative fans. (The Metropolitan Museum of Art)

Trading cards were already becoming something larger than sports. They were becoming tiny catalogs of popular culture.

The Card That Became a Legend

No discussion of early trading cards is complete without the T206 Honus Wagner.

Produced beginning in 1909 as part of the enormous T206 tobacco issue, the Wagner became the hobby’s most famous baseball card.

For decades, collectors repeated the story that Wagner demanded the card be withdrawn because he opposed tobacco. The real story is considerably more complicated.

Wagner used other tobacco products and even appeared in tobacco-related advertising. What is known is that Wagner objected to his inclusion and production of his card stopped early. Historians still debate his exact motivation.

The Baseball Hall of Fame points out another surprising fact: the T206 Wagner isn’t actually the rarest baseball card. Many cards exist in fewer numbers.

Its extraordinary status comes from a combination of rarity, Wagner’s greatness, history, publicity and generations of collectors regarding it as the hobby’s ultimate prize. (Baseball Hall of Fame)

Sometimes a card becomes valuable because it is rare. Sometimes it becomes valuable because it becomes legendary.

The Wagner became both.

Candy, Caramel and Cracker Jack

As America moved into the 20th century, trading cards increasingly escaped their association with tobacco.

Cards could be found with candy, caramel, cookies, bakery products, soft drinks and other consumer goods. The Smithsonian notes that cards have been distributed alongside everything from gum and baked goods to hot dogs. (National Postal Museum)

One famous example was Cracker Jack, whose baseball cards from the 1910s are now among the most recognizable cards of the prewar era.

The connection between food products and trading cards would eventually change the hobby forever.

Bubble Gum Creates a Generation of Collectors

During the 1930s, trading cards became increasingly associated with bubble gum.

Companies including Goudey produced colorful baseball cards that appealed directly to children. Babe Ruth, Lou Gehrig and other stars of the era suddenly became something kids could collect, trade and carry in their pockets.

The Smithsonian credits the arrival of bubble-gum cards during the Depression with helping trading cards achieve enormous popularity. (National Postal Museum)

The business model was brilliant. Children might have bought the product for the gum. Soon they were buying the gum for the cards.

That distinction would become enormously important.

1952: The Modern Baseball Card Arrives

One set more than almost any other established what generations would think a baseball card should look like: 1952 Topps.

Topps had experimented with baseball cards in 1951, but the following year Sy Berger and graphic artist Woody Gelman created something revolutionary.

The 407-card set featured large player images, team logos, facsimile autographs, statistics and biographical information. It was dramatically different from what had come before.

The National Baseball Hall of Fame credits the 1952 issue with helping transform card collecting into a major part of American popular culture and describes Berger as the “Father of the Modern Baseball Trading Card.” (Baseball Hall of Fame)

By 1954, Topps had adopted the familiar 2½-by-3½-inch dimensions that remain the standard trading-card size today. (Baseball Hall of Fame)

One of the Hobby’s Greatest Accidents

The most famous card in the 1952 set is Mickey Mantle #311.

Contrary to a common misconception, it isn’t Mantle’s rookie card. His major recognized rookie cards appeared in 1951. Yet the 1952 Topps Mantle became one of the world’s most valuable and recognizable sports cards.

Part of its legend comes from one extraordinary decision. Years after the set was released, Topps executive Sy Berger disposed of cases of unsold 1952 cards by having them dumped into the Atlantic Ocean.

Cards once considered excess inventory became treasures partly because so many disappeared forever. (Baseball Hall of Fame)

Few stories better illustrate one of collecting’s fundamental principles:

Today’s unwanted cardboard can become tomorrow’s treasure.

The Era of the Shoebox

From the 1950s through the 1970s, generations of children bought inexpensive packs, flipped cards against walls, traded duplicates with friends and stored collections in shoeboxes.

Cards ended up in bicycle spokes. Some were written on. Others were taped into albums. Corners were rounded by pockets and rubber bands.

Almost nobody imagined that pristine examples of those same cards might someday command extraordinary prices.

The phrase every longtime collector has heard—

“My mother threw my cards away.”

—became practically part of hobby folklore.

It also helps explain why condition matters so much today. The cards were originally produced to be enjoyed, not preserved as financial assets.

Competition Changes Everything

For decades, Topps dominated baseball cards. That began changing around 1980, when legal developments opened the market to additional competitors.

Fleer and Donruss entered the baseball-card battle, followed later by companies such as Score and Upper Deck. (Baseball Hall of Fame)

Competition encouraged innovation. Companies experimented with better photography, premium card stock, holograms, foil stamping and increasingly sophisticated designs.

Then, in 1989, Upper Deck helped redefine expectations for what a premium sports card could be.

Cards were no longer merely pieces of printed cardboard. They were becoming manufactured collectibles.

The Boom—and the Junk Wax Era

During the late 1980s and early 1990s, trading cards became a cultural phenomenon.

Card shops appeared everywhere. Shows filled hotel ballrooms, malls and convention centers. Price guides were treated almost like stock-market reports. Parents began putting unopened boxes away for their children.

The problem was supply.

Manufacturers produced enormous quantities of many sets to satisfy seemingly unlimited demand. Years later, collectors realized that many cards believed to be scarce had survived by the millions. The period became known among collectors as the “junk wax era.”

But dismissing the entire era misses something important.

It created millions of collectors.

Many of today’s hobby leaders, dealers, investors, breakers and lifelong enthusiasts opened their first packs during those years. And cards from stars such as Ken Griffey Jr., Michael Jordan, Bo Jackson and others remain among the most recognizable collectibles ever produced.

Cards Become Premium Collectibles

Beginning in the 1990s, manufacturers increasingly moved beyond the traditional base card. Collectors began chasing:

  • Inserts
  • Parallels
  • Refractors
  • Serial-numbered cards
  • Autographs
  • Game-used memorabilia cards
  • Short prints and super short prints
  • One-of-ones

Instead of simply asking “Do I have the player?” collectors now asked: “Which version?”

A single athlete might have hundreds—or thousands—of different cards produced during one season. Scarcity was increasingly created intentionally.

The Rise of Professional Grading

Condition had always mattered, but third-party grading transformed the marketplace.

Professional grading companies standardized evaluations of centering, corners, edges, surfaces and authenticity, sealing cards inside tamper-evident holders with numerical grades.

Suddenly a collector in California could buy an expensive card from a seller across the country with significantly more confidence about what was being purchased.

Population reports also introduced another dimension of scarcity. Collectors no longer cared only about how many cards were printed. They wanted to know: how many have received this grade?

A card might be common in raw form yet exceptionally scarce in the highest grades. Trading cards increasingly became both collectibles and documented assets.

Pokémon and the Trading Card Game Revolution

Sports were only one part of the story.

Trading cards had featured entertainment subjects for generations—from movie stars to comic characters—but another category transformed collecting dramatically: trading card games.

Magic: The Gathering helped establish the modern collectible trading-card-game model during the early 1990s. Pokémon soon became a worldwide collecting phenomenon.

Now cards had multiple identities simultaneously. They could be collected. They could be traded. They could be played competitively. And eventually, they could become extraordinarily valuable.

Pokémon demonstrated that a trading card did not need an athlete on the front to inspire passionate worldwide collecting. Yu-Gi-Oh!, One Piece, Disney Lorcana and numerous other games would expand that universe further.

Entertainment Cards Become Cultural Artifacts

Meanwhile, non-sports cards continued documenting popular culture.

Star Wars, Marvel, DC Comics, television shows, musicians, political figures, wrestling personalities and movie characters all appeared on cards.

That has produced an interesting modern concept: the pop-culture rookie card.

Collectors increasingly research the earliest recognized trading-card appearances of fictional characters, musicians, actors and cultural icons much as sports collectors seek rookie cards of athletes.

It represents a return to trading cards’ earliest roots. Remember—the tobacco cards of the 1880s weren’t exclusively about baseball either. They documented whatever fascinated society at the time.

The Internet Changes the Hobby

For most of trading-card history, collecting was local. You traded with friends, visited a card shop, attended shows, placed classified advertisements and called dealers from magazines.

The internet erased those boundaries.

Online marketplaces gave collectors access to cards anywhere in the world. Message boards and social media created enormous collecting communities. Price databases made sales history visible. Population reports provided scarcity data instantly. Auction houses could reach global bidders.

A collector looking for one obscure card produced 75 years earlier could potentially locate it in minutes.

The hobby had become global.

Breaking Changes the Pack

Another major development was the growth of group breaking.

Instead of purchasing an entire expensive box, collectors could buy a team, player or randomized position within a break and watch the product opened live online.

Breaking turned opening packs into entertainment. Collectors weren’t simply acquiring cards anymore. They were watching content, participating in communities and experiencing the suspense together.

YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, Whatnot and other platforms transformed collectors into broadcasters—and sometimes into celebrities within the hobby itself.

The Hobby Becomes an Alternative Asset Market

During the 21st century—and particularly during the collecting boom of the early 2020s—high-end cards began attracting attention far beyond traditional collectors.

Seven-figure card sales became international news. Auction houses expanded. Investment funds and fractional ownership appeared. Authentication, provenance, insurance, vault storage and sophisticated market analytics became increasingly important.

At the highest end, important trading cards began behaving more like fine art, rare coins and historical memorabilia.

But underneath the financial headlines remains the same emotional connection that existed generations ago. Collectors want to own something connected to a person, character, team, moment or memory that matters to them.

A Little-Known Giant of the Hobby: Jefferson Burdick

One of the most important people in trading-card history wasn’t a superstar athlete or card-company executive. He was a collector.

Jefferson R. Burdick developed the classification system that gave famous issues names such as T206.

Beginning in 1947, Burdick donated his enormous collection to New York’s Metropolitan Museum of Art. It included more than 30,000 baseball cards and hundreds of thousands of additional trade cards, postcards and other pieces of printed ephemera. (The Metropolitan Museum of Art)

The Met notes an especially remarkable piece of hobby lore: Burdick reportedly never attended a baseball game. (The Metropolitan Museum of Art)

Yet his work helped establish the organizational language collectors still use today.

It is a reminder that collectors themselves don’t merely preserve hobby history.

They create it.

More Than Cardboard

Today the term trading card encompasses an extraordinary range of collectibles: sports cards, gaming cards, entertainment cards, historical cards, promotional cards, autographs, relics, vintage tobacco issues, modern chrome cards, one-of-one masterpieces, digital collectibles—cards worth pennies and cards worth millions.

Some collectors build complete sets. Others chase rookies. Some specialize in one athlete, character, team, manufacturer or era. Some collect strictly for enjoyment. Others approach cards as investments. Most combine several of those motivations.

That diversity is one of the hobby’s greatest strengths.

The Next Chapter

Technology will continue changing trading cards.

Digital authentication, improved provenance tracking, advanced marketplaces, new forms of collectibles and entirely new ways to connect physical and digital ownership are likely to reshape the hobby again.

But the fundamental idea has survived every technological revolution.

A collector discovers something. There are others to find. Someone else has the one they need.

And suddenly a community forms around the chase.

From 19th-century advertising cards and tobacco packs to card shops, conventions, online marketplaces and livestreams reaching collectors around the world, trading cards have continuously reinvented themselves while preserving something remarkably simple: the thrill of the chase.

For more than a century and a half, trading cards have documented our athletes, entertainers, characters, heroes and culture. And every generation has added another chapter.

Preserving the Hobby’s Past. Strengthening Its Present. Building Its Future.

The National Trading Card Association exists at another important moment in that history.

As collecting becomes larger, more valuable, more technologically sophisticated and increasingly global, the hobby also faces new opportunities and challenges involving authenticity, education, ethics, standards, innovation, advocacy and preservation.

The NTCA’s mission is not simply about the cards themselves. It is about the collectors, dealers, manufacturers, graders, retailers, auction houses, content creators, shows and organizations that together make the hobby possible.

Trading cards have survived tobacco companies, world wars, economic depressions, changing generations, overproduction, new technologies and repeated predictions that collecting was dying. Instead, the hobby has continued to evolve.

The next chapter of trading-card history is being written now—and everyone in the hobby has an opportunity to be part of it.
