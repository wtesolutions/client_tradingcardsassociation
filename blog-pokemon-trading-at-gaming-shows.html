Bringing Pokémon Cards to Trade at Gaming Shows

By Eric Garrison, Technology Officer

Gaming conventions are no longer just dice, minis, and demo tables. Walk any show floor this year and you will see it: a kid in a Pikachu hoodie trading a sleeved Charizard across from a Magic draft, a dealer with a case of modern hits next to sealed booster boxes, parents comparing notes on which local shop still has Prismatic Evolutions.

Pokémon belongs at gaming shows. The question is not whether to bring cards. The question is how to bring them so the trades are fair, the cards stay safe, and everybody—especially new collectors—leaves wanting to come back.

Why a gaming show is different from a card show

A dedicated card show is built around binders, slabs, and comps. A gaming show is built around play. That changes the table.

  • Players want playable singles—trainers, energy, and deck staples—not only chase hits.
  • Families show up without a budget speech prepared. A five-dollar uncommon can make the whole day.
  • Dealers may not know TCGPlayer mid on a Japanese SAR. Have your numbers, and be kind when they do not.

That mix is the opportunity. You can move duplicates, fill a set, and introduce someone to the hobby in the same aisle.

What to bring

Pack light. You will walk more than you think.

  • A trade binder of cards you are actually willing to let go
  • A short want list—set, card number, and the most you will pay
  • Penny sleeves, a stack of toploaders, and a few team bags
  • A hard case or zip binder for anything that leaves the table with you
  • Cash in small bills, plus one backup payment method
  • A charged phone with recent sold listings already open
  • A written spending cap that includes parking, food, and admission

Leave the personal collection at home. The card you will not trade is the one someone will ask for—and the binder you would least like to lose in a crowded hall.

How to build the trade binder

Do this the night before, not in the parking lot.

  1. Pull only duplicates and cards you have already decided to part with. If you would be sad to see it go, it does not go in the binder.
  2. Sort by purpose, not by set. Front pages: fair trades and playable staples. Middle: mid-value hits that need a conversation. Back: higher-value cards in toploaders, clearly marked.
  3. Sleeve everything. Raw cards in a crowded aisle pick up whitening faster than you expect.
  4. Pencil a number on a slip behind each page—what you paid or the last sold you will accept. Signal dies in convention halls.
  5. Photograph the binder. If something walks, you have a record.

At the table

Ask first. Not every vendor wants to trade, and not every collector came to deal.

  • “Are you open to trades?” is a complete sentence.
  • Inspect both sides before you agree—front, back, corners, edges, centering.
  • Condition is the deal. A pack-fresh look and a binder-worn look are not the same card.
  • Comps are context, not a weapon. Recent solds help. They do not obligate anyone.
  • If the numbers do not work, walk away smiling. There is always another table.

Never leave a binder open on a table while you wander. Never flash a stack of cash in an aisle. Keep the case zipped when you are not using it.

Bringing kids

Gaming shows are one of the best classrooms the hobby has. Give them a small, fixed budget and let them choose a card they actually like. Resist the urge to turn it into a flip. The collector who has fun today is the collector who still has a binder in ten years.

If they want to trade, stand with them—do not negotiate for them unless they ask. Let them practice the sentence: “Would you take this for that?”

A simple plan for the first hour

  1. Find the show map and the Pokémon / TCG row.
  2. Walk the floor once without buying. Note two or three tables you want to revisit.
  3. Check your want list against what you saw. Adjust the cap if admission and food already spent it.
  4. Then sit down, open the binder, and start talking.

The show will still be there after you have walked it. The impulse buy in the first ten minutes is the one you regret in the parking lot.

See you on the floor

Trading is the oldest move in this hobby—older than slabs, older than apps, older than the modern set logo. Find it. Keep it. Trade it. Complete the set.

If you are heading to a gaming show this fall, bring the cards you are ready to let go, a list of what you still need, and enough patience for a long aisle. The NTCA calendar lists shows as we hear about them—and if you host one, submit the event.

We will see you between the demo tables and the singles cases.

— Eric Garrison, Technology Officer, National Trading Card Association
